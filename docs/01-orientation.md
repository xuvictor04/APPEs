# 01 — Orientation: How APPE Placement Actually Works

## The structure

APPEs are the fourth-year (P4) experiential block of the PharmD. ACPE Standards 2016
require **at least 36 weeks / 1,440 hours** of APPE, delivered in blocks that are usually
4–6 weeks each. Most programs run 8–10 blocks.

ACPE requires four **core** experiences:

| Core APPE | What it usually looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Community pharmacy | Chain or independent; MTM, immunizations, front-line counseling |
| Ambulatory patient care | Clinic-based; anticoagulation, diabetes, HTN, HIV, primary care |
| Hospital / health-system pharmacy | Operations, sterile compounding, order verification, formulary |
| Inpatient general medicine patient care | Rounding with a medicine team |

Everything else is **elective** — and that's the space this repo lives in. Your electives are
where you differentiate yourself for residency, fellowship, or a direct-to-industry job.

> **Important:** The exact core/elective split, block length, and how many electives you get
> are set by *your* school, not by ACPE. Pull your school's APPE manual before you plan.

## The three ways you get a rotation

### 1. School-assigned (the default)
Your OEE has affiliation agreements with a network of sites. You rank preferences in a
scheduling system (CORE ELMS, RxPreceptor, E*Value are common) and get assigned. Low effort,
low control, and — for industry/federal/association rotations — usually not enough.

### 2. Application-based national programs (the ones in this repo)
FDA, NIH, CDC, ASHP, AMCP, ACCP, AACP, NACDS, PQA, BMS, Boehringer Ingelheim, etc. run
their *own* competitive applications with their *own* deadlines, typically **12–18 months
before the rotation starts**. You apply directly, they select, and *then* your school sets up
the paperwork. These close early and quietly.

### 3. Student-initiated / cold outreach
You find a preceptor, email them, and they agree to host you. Your OEE then negotiates an
affiliation agreement. This is how most one-off industry and specialty rotations happen.
See [11 — Application Materials](11-application-materials.md) for the email template.

## The affiliation agreement is the real bottleneck

A site can want you and still be unable to take you. Before you invest in an application:

- **Ask your OEE whether an agreement already exists.** If it does, you're mostly clear.
- **If it doesn't**, ask how long a new one takes. Legal review between a university and a
  large pharma company or a federal agency can take **3–9 months**. Some schools simply will
  not start a new agreement for a single student.
- **Federal sites add layers**: background checks, fingerprinting, and onboarding can add
  2–4 months on top. Start earlier than feels reasonable.

Put the OEE conversation *before* the application, not after. It is the cheapest step and it
kills the most bad plans.

## Common eligibility gates

Recurring requirements across the programs in this repo:

- **U.S. citizenship** — required for FDA, CDC, NIH (in most cases), IHS, VA, USPHS, and
  many defense-adjacent sites. Non-citizens: see the international-student notes on each
  program's page; NIH has historically accommodated international students with extra
  processing time and added cancellation risk.
- **Enrollment in an ACPE-accredited program**, in good academic standing.
- **Active membership** in the sponsoring organization (ASHP, AMCP, ACCP, APhA all expect it).
- **A nomination or letter from your OEE** confirming scheduling and the affiliation agreement.
- **Immunizations, drug screen, background check, HIPAA/OSHA training** — assume all of it.
- **Unpaid.** Nearly every APPE is unpaid academic credit; you pay travel and housing.
  Paid federal externships (JRCOSTEP/SRCOSTEP) are a separate track — see
  [03 — Federal & Government](03-federal-government.md).

## How to build a portfolio, not a pile

A strong P4 year reads as a story. Some patterns that work:

**Industry / fellowship track**
Core four + industry APPE (BMS, BI, or similar) + FDA or NIH + drug information or medical
writing + a clinical rotation deep enough to defend at a fellowship interview.

**Regulatory / policy track**
Core four + FDA or USP + an association rotation (AMCP, ASHP, APhA, NACDS) + managed care
+ health policy or a state board.

**Academia track**
Core four + a teaching APPE at a college of pharmacy + a research/scholarship rotation +
two strong clinical specialties in your intended teaching area.

**Residency track (any specialty)**
Core four + 3–4 clinical specialties, ideally at least two at an academic medical center
with a residency program, plus one non-clinical rotation you can talk about intelligently.

Sequencing matters:
- Put a **strong clinical rotation early** if you need a letter of recommendation for
  residency applications (PhORCAS opens in the fall of P4).
- Put your **highest-stakes site** after you've had a couple of blocks to warm up.
- Avoid stacking your two hardest rotations back-to-back.

## Vocabulary you'll see in these docs

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **OEE / OEL** | Office of Experiential Education (or Learning) — your school's placement office |
| **Preceptor** | The licensed pharmacist supervising and grading you |
| **Affiliation agreement** | The legal contract between your school and the site |
| **Longitudinal APPE** | A rotation spread over months (e.g., one day/week) instead of a block |
| **PSEP** | Pharmacy Student Experiential Program — the name FDA and CDC both use |
| **COSTEP** | USPHS Commissioned Officer Student Training and Extern Program (paid) |
| **PhORCAS** | The residency application service — relevant to how you sequence APPEs |
